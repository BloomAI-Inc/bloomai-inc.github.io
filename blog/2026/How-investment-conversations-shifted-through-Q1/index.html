How Investment Conversations Shifted Through Q1 — And What It Tells Us About Where Things Are Heading

Created on June 04, 2026

2026   ·   investing   ai   private-markets   portfolio-strategy   q1-analysis   geopolitical-risk   ·   Markets

By Devansh Dutta · May 2026 · ~10 min read

From playing it safe in January to seriously talking about AI by March — here’s what actually changed this quarter.

If you had to sum up the first quarter in one line, it’d probably be this: everyone started the year a bit nervous, and ended it asking much bigger questions.

That’s not a bad thing. It’s actually a sign that the industry is adapting — moving from reactive to forward-looking, from “how do we protect what we have” to “where are the real opportunities from here.” But the shift didn’t happen all at once. It played out month by month, and tracking that arc is genuinely interesting.

Here’s what stood out across January, February, and March.

January

Cautious start — everyone was watching the same numbers

January felt a lot like the investment equivalent of looking both ways before crossing the road. Inflation hadn’t fully settled, rate decisions were still up in the air, and nobody wanted to make a big move before getting a clearer read on where things were going.

So naturally, a lot of the early conversations were about managing what you already have. Tax-efficient investing was a big one — not the most exciting topic, but a genuinely smart priority when returns are uncertain. Why give away more than you have to? ETFs came up a lot in this context too, as a clean, low-cost way to build or rebalance a portfolio without overcomplicating things.

Fixed income also made a bit of a comeback. Bond yields had moved enough to make the numbers work again for some investors, and after years of being largely ignored, that asset class was back in the conversation.

And underneath all of it — geopolitics. Energy markets, supply chains, commodity prices. Political risk wasn’t being treated as a fringe scenario anymore. It was becoming a baseline assumption that portfolios needed to account for.

February

Private markets moved from “interesting” to “actually let’s talk about this”

By February, the mood had shifted a little. The macro uncertainty hadn’t gone away — it never really does — but a more pressing question had started to take over: if public markets stay choppy, where do you actually find decent returns?

That’s when private markets started getting serious airtime. Private equity, private credit, and other alternatives went from being background topics to front-and-centre conversations. The appeal wasn’t just about chasing higher returns — it was more about building something that didn’t move in lockstep with every market swing. Uncorrelated. More resilient.

The appeal wasn’t just higher returns. It was about building portfolios that didn’t flinch every time the public market had a bad week.

There was also a lot more talk around wealth preservation and long-term planning — succession, intergenerational transfers, the kind of conversations that go beyond quarterly performance. Some of this was demographic, some of it was just a response to a world that keeps throwing curveballs.

Central banks were still a constant backdrop, with teams reading every policy signal they could find. And trade policy — tariffs, regulatory changes, shifting global supply chains — added more complexity that portfolios had to factor in.

March

AI stopped being a buzzword and became an actual investment question

March felt noticeably different from January. The conversations got bigger — less about managing risk, more about identifying opportunity. And one topic kept coming up: artificial intelligence.

Not in a vague, hand-wavy way. People were getting specific. Which sectors actually benefit? Which business models are at risk? How do you get exposure without overpaying for hype that might not materialise? These were the kinds of questions that a year ago would have felt premature. By March, they felt necessary.

That shift — from “AI is interesting” to “AI is something we need a position on” — is kind of a big deal. It suggests the industry is starting to treat technological transformation as a real input into investment decisions, not just a conference talking point.

Geopolitical risk stayed relevant, particularly around energy and commodities. And niche, specialised strategies picked up traction — investors looking for genuine diversification rather than the surface-level kind that looks good on paper but doesn’t actually hold up when things get rough.

Five things that ran through the whole quarter

Step back from the month-to-month detail and a few consistent threads stand out:

01 — The move away from traditional allocations kept accelerating. Standard 60/40 thinking isn’t dead, but it’s clearly not enough on its own anymore. Private markets and alternative strategies went from “nice to have” to a genuine part of the conversation.

02 — Risk management wasn’t a separate topic — it was baked into everything. Whether teams were talking inflation, geopolitics, or private credit, managing downside was always part of the equation.

03 — AI went from buzzword to investment framework. By the end of the quarter, it wasn’t being discussed as a trend to watch. It was being discussed as something that requires actual positioning.

04 — Tax efficiency stayed relevant all quarter. Not flashy, but consistent — a sign that investors are focused on net returns, not just gross performance.

05 — Global events kept reshaping the picture. Central bank decisions, tariff announcements, geopolitical flare-ups — none of these were treated as noise. They were actively being worked into investment thinking.

So where does that leave things going into Q2?

Honestly? With more questions than answers — but that’s not necessarily a bad place to be. The fact that conversations moved from “let’s protect what we have” to “where are the real opportunities” over the course of just three months suggests the industry is in an exploratory mode right now. Testing ideas, building frameworks, positioning for a landscape that’s clearly changing.

The themes that gained momentum this quarter — private markets, AI as an investment theme, genuine diversification, geopolitical risk as a baseline — aren’t going anywhere. If anything, they’ll probably be louder in Q2.

The teams that used this quarter to think through these questions properly will likely be better placed to act when clearer opportunities emerge. In markets, the groundwork usually matters more than the moment.
